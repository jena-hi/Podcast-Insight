# Real learning needs struggle you can't manufacture

We say we want people to grow. Then the second we watch them struggle, we jump in and take the pen out of their hand. We call it help. Most of the time, it is something else.

This week on Unscripted Intelligence, Arvin Ross and Dr. Michelle Ament sat down with Dr. Brian Arnold and Megan Freeman to get honest about productive struggle: what it is, when it actually works, and what we steal when we rescue too soon. It got personal fast. So let's get into the unavoidable truths.

## The rescue reflex is usually about us

Every person on the call confessed to the same crime. Parents doing the homework. Leaders fixing the work of the people they hired. Michelle put it bluntly: once her school grew and she started hiring, she did to her team exactly what she had done with her teenagers, stepping in to save them from the pain she had already lived through. It robbed them of the chance to do it their way, and it robbed her of learning there might be a better way.

Brian named the harder part. What we tell ourselves is benevolence can quietly be control, ego, or impatience. We get to be the hero, the catalyst, the one who made the difference, and that feels good. Here is where it gets sharp for our moment: AI now threatens to remove a lot of that struggle, and with it, the gratification of being the rescuer. If our sense of purpose depends on saving people, an AI-driven world is going to test us.

Arvin said the quiet part out loud. When he traced his own urge to help, only about 20 percent was love. The other 80 percent was the need to be in control. That is the reflex. Naming it is the first step to interrupting it.

## Not all struggle is worth it

Struggle is not automatically good. Brian gave the cleanest test we have heard: it is only productive if there is a real chance it leads to growth. Flapping your arms will not make you fly no matter how long you try, so that is not productive struggle, it is just suffering. The magic ingredient is a genuine opportunity for success.

Arvin raised him one. Productive struggle also needs a hook, something inside the difficulty that keeps a person fighting instead of quitting. That is how resilience gets built, one reason to keep going at a time.

Then came the distinction that reframes the whole conversation: designed struggle versus authentic struggle. Designed struggle is the problem a teacher sets, with a path they expect you to walk. Authentic struggle is messier, the bullying, the impossible problem, the moment with no clear route. Both can be productive, but only when the human actually changes: asking better questions, transferring what they learned, showing up with a different mindset than they had before.

## The conditions that make struggle pay off

If we want people to struggle well, we have to build the conditions for it. Three kept surfacing.

First, psychological safety. People take risks, disagree, and admit they do not have the answer only when it is safe to do so. We rarely build that in classrooms, in workplaces, or honestly in our wider culture.

Second, respectful disagreement. Brian asked where any of us actually learn to disagree well, and answered it himself: almost nowhere, because real discourse does not fit on a bumper sticker. What gets modeled instead is fighting and backstabbing. Arvin tied that to something he is intense about: we have stopped making space for opposing ideas, and that absence is feeding a brittleness we keep seeing in our kids.

Third, the willingness to not save. Megan reminded us that walking in with the Mary Poppins bag, ready to pull out every fix, robs people of the ability to solve things themselves. The move that matters is refusing to lower the bar, especially for the learners who have already struggled the most, because lowering it strips them of the tools they will need.

Megan also gave us the frame we keep coming back to. The word "teach" traces to a root that means to draw out, to draw from a well. The art is not handing someone the answer. It is putting them in a position to draw the answer out of themselves, then having the nerve to stay quiet while they do.

## The unavoidable truth

A line from the live chat, from Jason Blair, stopped the room: when a student is struggling, that is the moment they are trying to become who they are meant to be. Do not rob them of it.

So the next time you feel the urge to step in, sit with one question first: what would I be stealing? Sometimes the kindest, bravest thing we can do is slow down, stay close, and let the struggle do its work.

That is the whole point of this movement. The skills that make us human do not get handed to us. We build them, usually in the exact moments we are tempted to skip.
